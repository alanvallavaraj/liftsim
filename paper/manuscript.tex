\section*{Abstract}
Passengers with access requirements, such as wheelchair users, may be given priority when boarding a lift. This study quantifies what such a policy costs other passengers and tests whether destination control can honour it. An open discrete-event lift simulator, verified by automated tests and agreeing with the classical up-peak round-trip time within 2\%, compares five group controllers, two boarding rules and two priority-passenger profiles in 31,680 paired runs. Every measured passenger is followed for at least 900~s, so a mean waiting time restricted to 600~s is observed exactly. With landing-call control, priority-first boarding adds 1.8~s to other passengers' restricted mean wait at the reference demand (the up-peak handling capacity) and saves priority passengers 9.3~s. Under destination control, priority at the door has no measurable effect up to the reference demand, because each passenger may board only the allocated car. A priority-aware allocation rule (DCS-P0) reduces priority passengers' wait by 30\% to 43\% there while adding 0.6 to 4.1~s for others, but under sustained overload it raises the all-passenger mean by 29.9~s. Passengers who need more space reduce capacity for everyone by more than boarding order changes any wait.

\section*{Practical application}
Lift designers and building operators can adopt priority-first boarding for passengers with access requirements at little cost to other passengers while demand stays below the up-peak handling capacity; the cost grows in heavily loaded periods and with the share of priority passengers. With destination control, a boarding rule alone gives no benefit in normal operation: the priority label must be used when cars are allocated. Where many passengers need extra space, capacity planning should count them in places, not persons. The results come from a model with illustrative parameters and indicate direction and relative size, not design values.

\paragraph{Keywords} lift group control, destination control, discrete-event simulation, priority boarding, accessibility, restricted mean waiting time


\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Lift group controllers are designed and compared by simulation. Design guidance treats simulation as a standard companion to the classical round-trip-time calculation \cite{cibse2025,iso8100,barney2016}, and research on group control relies on simulated waiting times to compare controllers \cite{siikonen1993,crites1998,utgoff2012,wan2024}.

Some passengers have access requirements that make waiting and boarding harder, for example wheelchair users and people with reduced mobility. Accessibility of lifts is addressed by EN~81-70 \cite{en8170}, which concerns the design of the lift (for example car dimensions and controls). Whether such passengers should also be served ahead of others is a separate, operational question. Han et al.\ \cite{han2024} propose an elevator priority scheduling system that recognises wheelchair users by image analytics and gives passengers with mobility impairment a higher priority weight. Priority scheduling for lifts is thus not new. What is missing is a systematic account of what a priority policy costs the other passengers, how that cost depends on demand and on the type of group control, and whether the policy works at all under destination control, where each passenger is allocated to one car before boarding.

The simplest policy is \emph{priority-first boarding}: at a landing, passengers flagged for priority board before others. If a car has room for everyone, boarding order cannot change any waiting time; a cost to others can arise only when a car leaves passengers behind. For a work-conserving single-server queue, the conservation law states that a priority discipline redistributes waiting without changing a load-weighted total \cite{kleinrock1965,schrage1970}. A lift group is neither single-server nor work-conserving in this sense, because boarding order changes car loads, stops and routes, so the size of the effect has to be measured.

Measuring it near and above handling capacity raises a methodological issue. Some passengers are still waiting when observation ends. An average over served passengers then omits those with the longest waits, and two policies that leave different numbers of people behind are compared on different populations. Survival analysis handles such censored outcomes with the restricted mean, an average of times capped at a fixed horizon \cite{royston2013}; simulation texts warn that the observation window shapes what an estimator measures \cite{law2015}.

This paper addresses three questions.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2em,style=nextline,itemsep=1pt]
\item[Q1] What does priority-first boarding cost other passengers and give priority passengers, as a function of demand, controller and the physical needs of the priority passenger?
\item[Q2] Under destination control, does using the priority label in car allocation give priority passengers more than priority at the door, and at what cost to others?
\item[Q3] Does the conventional served-passenger average rank controllers in the same order as a capped mean over all arrivals?
\end{description}

The contributions are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt]
\item An open, seconds-based discrete-event simulator of a lift group with explicit kinematics, door and transfer times, passengers of different sizes, five group controllers and optional priority-first boarding, with automated verification tests and an analytic check against the classical up-peak round-trip time (Section~\ref{sec:vv}).
\item A paired experimental design in which every measured passenger is followed for at least 900~s, so that a capped mean waiting time over all arrivals is observed without estimation (Section~\ref{sec:design}).
\item A quantification of the cost and benefit of priority-first boarding across demand levels, traffic patterns, controllers and two priority-passenger profiles (Section~\ref{sec:res-prio}).
\item The observation that priority at the door has no measurable effect under destination control below saturation, and a priority-aware allocation rule in two variants (DCS-P1, DCS-P0) with a fully specified algorithm, evaluated for benefit, cost, tail behaviour and overload (Sections~\ref{sec:dcsp} and~\ref{sec:res-dcs}--\ref{sec:res-fair}).
\end{enumerate}
We do not claim a new optimisation principle. DCS-P is a specific formulation within the familiar family of cost-based immediate allocation: it combines a class-dependent weight on the delay imposed on others with a class-dependent capacity test and immediate reallocation of displaced passengers (Section~\ref{sec:dcsp}). To our knowledge, its effect, and the ineffectiveness of door priority under destination control, have not been quantified in the open literature. All code, the test plan and run-level results are public \cite{dataset}; the model is reported following the STRESS-DES guidelines \cite{monks2019} (\ref{app:stress}). A plain-language summary and a glossary of terms are provided as supplementary material.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Traffic calculation and simulation} Classical design estimates the up-peak round-trip time (RTT) of a car from the expected number of stops and the highest reversal floor, and derives the interval and the five-minute handling capacity \cite{barney2016}. Al-Sharif et al.\ evaluate the RTT by Monte Carlo simulation under up-peak traffic and conventional control \cite{alsharif2012}. Simulation is part of current design guidance \cite{cibse2025,iso8100}. Siikonen's early simulation study describes the traffic model and control of a lift group simulator \cite{siikonen1993}.

\paragraph{Group control} With conventional control a landing call (up or down) is allocated to a car. Published approaches include reinforcement learning \cite{crites1998,wan2024}, real-time combinatorial optimisation \cite{utgoff2012} and learning from real-time occupancy \cite{wang2021}. The estimated-time-to-destination (ETD) principle allocates a call by the new passenger's expected time to destination together with the delay imposed on passengers already allocated \cite{smith2002}. With destination control, passengers register their destination at the landing and are allocated to a car before boarding; the problem has been treated by AI planning \cite{koehler2002}, by exact optimisation for a single car \cite{tanaka2005} and with uncertain passenger arrivals \cite{sorsa2018}.

\paragraph{Priority and fairness} Cameras have been used to adapt group control to the number of waiting passengers \cite{kimmoon2001} and to dispatch cars fairly as well as effectively \cite{yamauchi2019}. The closest work is that of Han et al.\ \cite{han2024}. Their elevator priority scheduling system uses deep-learning image analytics to recognise wheelchair users, assigns priority weights according to the level of passenger need, and is reported to give people with special needs a lower average waiting time than traditional scheduling. Their contribution lies in detecting such passengers and in showing that weighting helps them. The present study is complementary. It takes the label as given and (i) quantifies, with uncertainty, both the benefit to priority passengers and the cost to other passengers across demand levels; (ii) compares landing-call control with destination control; (iii) shows that priority applied only at the door has little effect under destination control; (iv) specifies a priority-aware destination allocation mechanism and evaluates its two variants, DCS-P1 and DCS-P0; and (v) reports sensitivity to priority share and passenger properties, behaviour under overload and the resulting trade-off between priority and other passengers. In queueing theory, conservation laws constrain what any priority discipline can achieve in a single-server queue \cite{kleinrock1965,schrage1970}. A car journey of length $D$ takes
\begin{equation}
t_f(D)=t_{sd}+\begin{cases} D/v + v/a, & D\ge v^2/a,\\ 2\sqrt{D/a}, & D<v^2/a,\end{cases}
\label{eq:flight}
\end{equation}
with rated speed $v$, equal acceleration and deceleration $a$ and start delay $t_{sd}=0.5$~s; jerk is not modelled. Doors open in $t_o=2.0$~s and close in $t_c=3.0$~s. Each passenger transfer takes $t_p=1.2$~s; passengers alight and then board, one at a time. A car admits passengers up to a boarding limit $Q$ of 80\% of the rated load, the car loading assumed in up-peak calculation \cite{barney2016}. Figure~\ref{fig:stop} illustrates one journey and one stop.

\caption{One journey of three floors and one stop in building B1: car speed (top) and the sequence of events with their durations (bottom).}\label{fig:stop}\end{figure}

\subsection{Demand and the reference demand level}\label{sec:demand}
Passengers arrive singly as a Poisson process of rate $\lambda$. Each is incoming (lobby to a uniformly chosen upper floor), outgoing (upper floor to lobby) or interfloor (two distinct upper floors). The traffic patterns have incoming/outgoing/interfloor shares of 0.85/0.10/0.05 (up-peak), 0.40/0.40/0.20 (lunch) and 0.05/0.85/0.10 (down-peak). Demand is expressed as
\begin{equation}
x=\frac{300\,\lambda}{\mathrm{HC5}},
\label{eq:load}
\end{equation}
the arrival rate relative to the classical up-peak handling capacity of Table~\ref{tab:buildings}. We call $x$ the \emph{demand level} and $x=1$ the \emph{reference demand}. The same normalisation is applied to all patterns so that they can be compared at equal passenger flow. It is a reference, not a saturation point: HC5 is derived for pure incoming traffic, collective control and cars filled to $Q$. Two-way traffic and destination control saturate at higher flows. In this study, lunch traffic still serves all measured passengers at $x=1.3$, and destination control passes $x=1$ with moderate waits (Section~\ref{sec:res-service}). We therefore describe conditions by their demand level and by observed service, and use ``overload'' only where waits grow through the run.

Steady scenarios hold $\lambda$ constant. Time-varying scenarios use the up-peak mix with demand rising linearly from $x=0.5$ to a peak after 1,800~s, falling linearly to 0.5 at 3,600~s and remaining there (Figure~\ref{fig:ramp}).

\subsection{Passengers with priority}\label{sec:profiles}
Each passenger receives an independent uniform draw $u_i$ from a random stream separate from the arrival stream, and is a priority passenger when $u_i<s$ for priority share $s$. Arrival times, origins and destinations are therefore identical for every share and boarding rule. Passenger $i$ occupies $s_i$ places and needs transfer time $t_{p,i}$. Two profiles are used in the main campaign.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Simple priority} ($s=0.15$): $s_i=1$, $t_{p,i}=t_p$. The passenger is entitled to board first but has no additional physical needs.
\item \emph{Wheelchair-like} ($s=0.05$): $s_i=3$ places, $t_{p,i}=3t_p=3.6$~s. These values are illustrative assumptions, not measurements, and are not meant to describe all wheelchair users. They represent a passenger who needs substantially more floor area and time than a standing passenger.
\end{itemize}
The two profiles differ in share and in physical properties; a supplementary experiment separates these factors (Section~\ref{sec:res-sens}). Priority-first boarding is an operational policy examined here. It is not a requirement of EN~81-70, and the model assumes that the label is known without error and that other passengers comply.

\subsection{Car movement}
Cars follow directional-collective logic. A car continues in its direction of travel while it has car calls or allocated landing stops ahead, stops at car calls and at allocated stops for its direction of travel, and at its farthest stop serves a call at that floor before reversing. A moving car accepts a new stop only at floors where it can still decelerate. An idle car returns to the lobby and can be reallocated on the way. While doors are open, passengers arriving at that landing for the serving direction board if they fit. A car at its boarding limit does not stop for landing calls. If the free space of a car is smaller than the space needed by the next waiting passenger, later passengers who fit may board.

\subsection{Baseline controllers}\label{sec:controllers}
All controllers share the car model and differ in allocation (Table~\ref{tab:controllers}). With landing-call control (NC, ETA, ETD, ETDQ) a registered call is allocated to one car; a passenger boards any car that opens at the landing for the right direction, and a car that cannot take the waiting passengers hands the call to another car. With destination control (DCS) each passenger is allocated to a car on arrival and boards only that car.

\subsection{Priority-aware destination control (DCS-P)}\label{sec:dcsp}

\paragraph{State and notation} At decision time $t$ the controller knows, for each car $c\in C$: its position, direction and door state; its load $\ell_c$ in places; the passengers on board with their destinations; and for each landing and direction $(f,\delta)$ the ordered list $A_c(f,\delta)$ of passengers allocated to $c$ and still waiting, in order of allocation. Passenger $i$ has origin $o_i$, destination $g_i$, direction $\delta_i$, size $s_i$ and priority flag $\pi_i\in\{0,1\}$.

\paragraph{Route plan} For car $c$ the controller builds a plan: the sequence of stops implied by directional-collective movement for the car's car calls, pickups and the drop-off floors of passengers not yet on board, with estimated stop times $\tau_k$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:flight} and a dwell of $t_o+t_c$ plus the transfer times of the passengers expected to alight or board. Each stop $k$ has a weight $w_k$, the number of passengers who alight or board there. The plan is built twice, without and with passenger $i$, giving stop times $\tau_k$ and $\tau'_k$, the estimated arrival time $T_i(c)$ of $i$ at $g_i$, and $L_i(c)$, the planned load of the car in places immediately after boarding at $i$'s pickup stop.

\paragraph{Cost} Passenger $i$ is allocated to the car minimising
\begin{equation}
J_i(c)=\bigl(T_i(c)-t\bigr)+\alpha_i\sum_{k} w_k\,\pos{\tau'_k-\tau_k}+M\,\mathbf{1}\bigl[\Lambda_i(c)>Q\bigr]+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{idx}(c),
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
with $M=600$~s and $\varepsilon=10^{-6}$~s. The first term is the passenger's own estimated time to destination, the second the delay imposed on passengers already allocated to or travelling in the car, the third a penalty when the planned load exceeds the boarding limit, and the fourth breaks ties in favour of the lower car index. Standard DCS uses $\alpha_i=1$ and $\Lambda_i(c)=L_i(c)$ for every passenger.

\paragraph{The two priority-aware elements} For a general passenger ($\pi_i=0$) DCS-P is identical to DCS. For a priority passenger:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Class-dependent capacity test.} General passengers allocated to the same car at the same pickup will board after the priority passenger, so they are not counted against the limit:
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_i(c)=L_i(c)-\sum_{j\in A_c(o_i,\delta_i),\ \pi_j=0}s_j .
\label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}
\item \emph{Class-dependent weight on others' delay.} $\alpha_i=1$ in DCS-P1 and $\alpha_i=0$ in DCS-P0.
\end{enumerate}
After the allocation to $c^\ast$, if $L_i(c^\ast)>Q$, general passengers are displaced at once: starting with the most recently allocated, passengers with $\pi_j=0$ are removed from $A_{c^\ast}(o_i,\delta_i)$ until the removed places reach $L_i(c^\ast)-Q$ or no general passenger remains at that pickup. Each displaced passenger is re-allocated by the standard rule over the other cars. Displacement does not cascade: a re-allocated general passenger displaces nobody. Algorithm~\ref{alg:dcsp} states the procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{DCS-P allocation of an arriving passenger $i$ at time $t$ (DCS-P1: $\alpha^{P}=1$; DCS-P0: $\alpha^{P}=0$)}\label{alg:dcsp}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{Allocate}{$i$, excluded car $e$ (optional)}
\State $c^\ast\gets\varnothing$;\ $J^\ast\gets\infty$
\ForAll{cars $c\in C$, $c\neq e$ (if $|C|>1$)}
  \State build plan of $c$ without $i$: stop times $\tau_k$, weights $w_k$
  \State build plan of $c$ with $i$: stop times $\tau'_k$, arrival $T_i(c)$, load $L_i(c)$
  \State $\alpha\gets\alpha^{P}$ if $\pi_i=1$, else $1$
  \State $\Lambda\gets L_i(c)-\sum_{j\in A_c(o_i,\delta_i),\,\pi_j=0}s_j$ if $\pi_i=1$, else $L_i(c)$
  \State $J\gets T_i(c)-t+\alpha\sum_k w_k\pos{\tau'_k-\tau_k}+M\,\mathbf{1}[\Lambda>Q]+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{idx}(c)$
  \If{$J<J^\ast$} $c^\ast\gets c$;\ $J^\ast\gets J$;\ $L^\ast\gets L_i(c)$ \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $(c^\ast,L^\ast)$
\EndFunction
\Statex
\Procedure{OnArrival}{$i$}
\State $(c^\ast,L^\ast)\gets$ \Call{Allocate}{$i$};\ append $i$ to $A_{c^\ast}(o_i,\delta_i)$
\If{$\pi_i=1$ \textbf{and} $L^\ast>Q$}
  \State $E\gets L^\ast-Q$;\ $D\gets[\,]$
  \While{$E>0$ \textbf{and} $A_{c^\ast}(o_i,\delta_i)$ contains a general passenger}
     \State $j\gets$ most recently allocated general passenger in $A_{c^\ast}(o_i,\delta_i)$
     \State remove $j$ from $A_{c^\ast}(o_i,\delta_i)$;\ append $j$ to $D$;\ $E\gets E-s_j$
  \EndWhile
  \ForAll{$j\in D$, earliest allocated first}
     \State $(c',\cdot)\gets$ \Call{Allocate}{$j$, $c^\ast$};\ append $j$ to $A_{c'}(o_j,\delta_j)$
  \EndFor
\EndIf
\EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Operational rules shared with DCS} At a stop the passengers in $A_c(f,\delta)$ board in priority-first order (or in arrival order under FCFS), skipping any passenger who does not fit. When the doors close, passengers of that landing who could not board because the car is full, or because none of them fits, are re-allocated with \textsc{Allocate} excluding that car. If a plan does not reach a stop within its horizon, a fallback time of $10^4$~s is used, which makes that car unattractive.

\paragraph{Relation to existing rules and complexity} Compared with DCS, DCS-P changes only the allocation of priority passengers and adds displacement. Compared with priority-first boarding, which acts when the doors open, DCS-P acts when the passenger registers. Weighting passenger classes differently in a dispatching cost is a known device; the specific elements here are the capacity test of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda} and immediate displacement, which together let a priority passenger take a place in the next suitable car without leaving a general passenger to discover this at the door. DCS-P1 and DCS-P0 are the two ends of one parameter, $\alpha^{P}\in[0,1]$. One allocation builds two plans per car. A plan handles at most $O(N)$ distinct stops and selects the next stop by a linear scan, so an allocation costs $O\bigl(|C|\,(N^2+m)\bigr)$ operations, where $m$ is the number of passengers currently allocated to or travelling in a car; a displacement adds one allocation per displaced passenger.

\subsection{Boarding rules}
Under first-come-first-served boarding (FCFS) waiting passengers board in arrival order. Under priority-first boarding (PFB) priority passengers board first in arrival order, then the others (Figure~\ref{fig:boarding}). With destination control only passengers allocated to the car are eligible. No baseline controller uses the priority label in allocation.

\subsection{Implementation}
The simulator is an event-driven Python program (Python 3.13, NumPy). Events are passenger arrival, car arrival at a floor, doors open, transfer end and doors closed; stale car-arrival events are invalidated by a version counter when a car is re-routed. Random numbers come from NumPy \texttt{SeedSequence} objects keyed by building, scenario and replication. The simulator and analysis programs were written with the assistance of generative AI coding tools (see the declaration at the end of the paper); their correctness rests on the tests and checks of Section~\ref{sec:vv} and on the released code, not on the tools.

\section{Verification and validation}\label{sec:vv}
Following Sargent \cite{sargent2013}, we separate verification from validation.

\subsection{Verification}
The released test suite contains 15 automated tests, all of which pass. They check the following.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Kinematics.} Flight times equal the closed form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:flight} in both regimes; the deceleration point precedes arrival.
\item \emph{Event timing.} For a single passenger the simulated boarding and alighting times equal hand calculations to floating-point precision, for a passenger at the lobby (wait 0; journey $t_o+t_p+t_c+t_f(5h)+t_o+t_p$), for a passenger five floors up (wait $t_f(5h)$) under every controller, and for a slow, three-place passenger.
\item \emph{Capacity.} Eleven simultaneous passengers and a limit of ten: ten board and the eleventh waits for the car's return. In random runs the load in places never exceeds $Q$.
\item \emph{Passenger logic.} Nobody boards before arriving, boards twice or alights without boarding; passengers board only cars serving their direction at their origin; destination-control passengers board only the allocated car and, under FCFS, in arrival order; at the end of a run, passengers waiting plus passengers boarded equal passengers arrived.
\item \emph{Liveness.} In light traffic every passenger is served, and no car makes more than twelve consecutive floor arrivals without opening. This test was added after an earlier version of the direction rule was found to let a car skip a call at its reversal floor; all results in this paper were produced after the correction.
\item \emph{Arrival process.} Counts match the Poisson mean, the traffic mix matches its specification, and streams are reproducible from their seed keys.
\item \emph{Invariance.} FCFS results do not depend on the priority share; PFB with no priority passengers equals FCFS; DCS-P1 with no priority passengers equals DCS.
\item \emph{Measures.} The outcome functions reproduce a hand-computed example with a censored passenger.
\end{itemize}
A separate check of 39,588 journeys found none shorter than the physical minimum implied by Eq.~\eqref{eq:flight}.

\section{Experimental design}\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Main campaign}
The main campaign crosses three buildings with 22 demand scenarios: three traffic patterns at six steady demand levels ($x=0.5$, 0.7, 0.85, 1.0, 1.15, 1.3) and four time-varying scenarios (peaks 1.0, 1.15, 1.3, 1.5), giving 66 situations. Each has 20 replications. The five baseline controllers are run in four modes: FCFS and PFB for the simple-priority profile, and FCFS and PFB for the wheelchair-like profile. DCS-P1 and DCS-P0 are run with PFB for both profiles. This gives $66\times20\times(5\times4+2\times2)=31{,}680$ runs. Within a situation and replication every controller and mode sees the identical passenger stream (common random numbers \cite{law2015}). The grid, outcomes and analyses were fixed in a protocol before execution, and no run was excluded. One correction was made after execution: under destination control with FCFS boarding, passengers had boarded in order of allocation to the car, which differs from arrival order for passengers re-allocated after being left behind. The rule was corrected to arrival order and the 2,640 affected runs (DCS with FCFS) were re-executed on the same passenger streams; this is recorded as an amendment to the protocol. Aggregated intervals were added at the same time. DCS-P and ETDQ were developed on separate pilot streams that are not part of the campaign.

\subsection{Observation scheme and outcomes}\label{sec:outcomes}
Passengers arrive during $[0,4500)$~s and the system is observed until $T=4500$~s. The measured cohort $\mathcal{P}$ contains passengers arriving in $[300,3600)$~s (Figure~\ref{fig:window}). The first 300~s allow cars, which start empty at the lobby, to distribute; arrivals continue after the measured window so that congestion does not ease artificially. Every measured passenger is followed for at least 900~s.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=.9\linewidth]{pic_window.pdf}
\caption{Observation scheme and the treatment of waits in $R_{600}$.}\label{fig:window}\end{figure}

For passenger $i$ with arrival time $a_i$ and boarding time $b_i$ (the instant the doors of the boarded car begin to open, or $a_i$ if the doors are already open), the observed wait is
\begin{equation}
W_i=\begin{cases} b_i-a_i, & b_i\le T,\\ T-a_i, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
\label{eq:wait}
\end{equation}
The primary outcome is the capped (restricted) mean waiting time
\begin{equation}
R_{h}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{i\in \mathcal{P}}\min(W_i,h),\qquad h=600~\text{s}.
\label{eq:rmwt}
\end{equation}
Because $T-a_i\ge 900>h$ for every measured passenger, $\min(W_i,h)$ is known exactly whether or not the passenger boards; no estimation from censored data is needed. The conventional comparator is the served-passenger average
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AWT}_{\mathrm{s}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_b|}\sum_{i\in \mathcal{P}_b}W_i,\qquad \mathcal{P}_b=\{i\in \mathcal{P}: b_i\le T\}.
\label{eq:awt}
\end{equation}
To separate exclusion of unserved passengers from capping, $R_{600}$ over served passengers only ($R_{600}^{\mathrm{s}}$) is also computed, as are $R_{300}$, $R_{900}$ and the served fraction. Outcomes are computed for all passengers, for priority passengers ($u_i<s$) and for the others ($u_i\ge s$); these groups are defined by the same draws under FCFS and PFB, so contrasts are paired passenger by passenger.

$R_{600}$ has two limitations that matter for interpretation. It says nothing about how long waits beyond 600~s last, so under sustained overload, where many waits reach the cap, it understates differences between policies. And under steady demand above saturation the queue grows through the run, so levels of any waiting-time measure depend on the length of the measured window (Section~\ref{sec:res-agree}).

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
The unit of resampling is the replication. For a situation, controller pair or mode pair, the effect is the mean over the 20 replications of the paired difference. Aggregated effects are means over cells (building $\times$ scenario $\times$ controller). Intervals are 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals from 4,000 resamples: replications are resampled within each situation, with the same indices for every controller and mode of that situation (they share passenger streams) and independently across situations. An effect is called \emph{clear} when its interval excludes zero.

The pre-specified primary comparisons are the aggregated effects by demand level for Q1 (Table~\ref{tab:prio}) and Q2 (Table~\ref{tab:dcs}), and the count of discordant controller pairs for Q3. Cell-level results (one per building, scenario and controller) are reported descriptively. Intervals are not adjusted for multiplicity; with this many comparisons, isolated cell-level intervals that just exclude zero should not be read as findings, and we base conclusions on aggregated effects, their size relative to baseline waits, and their consistency across demand levels. Intervals reflect replication variability in the model and say nothing about model error.

\subsection{Supplementary experiments}\label{sec:supp}
Two experiments were added after the main campaign had been analysed; they are exploratory and reported separately. Both reuse replications 0--9 of the main campaign's passenger streams under steady demand (8,730 runs).
\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2em,style=nextline,itemsep=1pt]
\item[S1: distribution and overload] At $x=0.85$, 1.0, 1.15 and 1.3, simple-priority profile: ETDQ and DCS under FCFS and PFB, DCS-P1, DCS-P0 and an adaptive variant, DCS-PA, which applies DCS-P0 while the number of passengers waiting in the building is at most $\theta\,|C|\,Q$ and DCS-P1 otherwise ($\theta=0.5$, chosen on pilot streams from $\{0.5,1,2\}$). Additional outcomes are the share of passengers waiting more than 300~s and the 95th percentile of the wait. $R_{600}$ is also recomputed for measured windows $[600,3600)$ and $[300,3300)$.
\item[S2: sensitivity] At $x=0.85$, 1.0 and 1.15 under ETD, DCS and DCS-P0: priority shares of 5\%, 10\% and 15\% for one-place passengers; and, at a share of 5\%, passengers needing three places only, three times the transfer time only, or both.
\end{description}

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Service levels under first-come-first-served boarding}\label{sec:res-service}
Figure~\ref{fig:service} shows $R_{600}$ and $\mathrm{AWT}_{\mathrm{s}}$ for the steady scenarios. Averaged over buildings and controllers, up-peak $R_{600}$ is 9.6~s at $x=0.5$, 20.8~s at $x=0.85$, 48.8~s at $x=1$ and 360~s at $x=1.3$, where 96\% of measured passengers are served. Lunch traffic remains unsaturated on this scale (65.8~s at $x=1.3$, all served), and down-peak lies between (116.5~s, 97\% served). Averaged over patterns, ETA and ETDQ give the shortest waits at $x=0.5$ (14.7 and 15.0~s; ETD 15.5, DCS 18.9, NC 19.4). At $x=1$ ETDQ is best (38.8~s) and DCS second (41.5~s). Above it, destination control is clearly best in the mean (59.0~s at $x=1.15$ against 109 to 144~s; 118.4~s at $x=1.3$ against 167 to 222~s), consistent with the higher handling capacity generally attributed to it in incoming traffic \cite{barney2016,smith2002}. In the time-varying scenarios all measured passengers are served at every peak level, and $R_{600}$ averaged over controllers is 20.8, 40.1, 95.0 and 195.3~s for peaks of 1.0 to 1.5.

\emph{Below the reference demand} the policy is nearly neutral. At $x=0.85$ others wait 0.3~s longer and priority passengers 1.1~s less; the largest cost in any single building--pattern--controller cell at $x<1$ is 3.3~s. \emph{At the reference demand} others lose 1.8~s and priority passengers gain 9.3~s, 21\% of their FCFS wait. \emph{Above it} both grow: at $x=1.15$ others lose 14.5~s (11\% of their wait) and priority passengers gain 81.1~s (63\%). The cost arises where cars leave passengers behind. In steady up-peak traffic others lose 5.3~s (3.8 to 6.8) at $x=1$ and 40.2~s (36.6 to 43.8) at $x=1.15$; in lunch traffic at most 1.7~s at any level; in down-peak 3.1~s at $x=1.15$. A limited period above the reference demand is enough to produce a cost: in the time-varying scenarios others lose 0.9, 4.4, 14.6 and 31.4~s for peaks of 1.0 to 1.5, and priority passengers gain 2.5, 18.7, 76.0 and 186.1~s.

A distinct effect is much larger than that of boarding order: the mere presence of 5\% wheelchair-like passengers, under FCFS, raises $R_{600}$ of all passengers by 2.9~s (2.6 to 3.2) at $x=0.85$, 21.7~s (19.8 to 23.7) at $x=1$ and 39.9~s (38.1 to 41.9) at $x=1.15$, averaged over the five baseline controllers, because each car carries fewer people and dwells longer.

\emph{Door priority has no measurable effect until destination control saturates.} Up to $x=1$ it changes no group's $R_{600}$ by more than 0.2~s in any cell. \section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Answers to the research questions}
\emph{Q1.} With landing-call control, priority-first boarding costs other passengers little as long as the group is not saturated: at most 3.3~s in any cell below the reference demand and 1.8~s on average at it. It then yields a moderate gain for priority passengers (9.3~s). Its value lies in busy periods, where gains are large; a peak of 1.15 times the reference demand lasting part of an hour gives priority passengers 18.7~s for a cost of 4.4~s to others. The cost to others scales with the share of priority passengers.

\emph{Q2.} Under destination control, priority at the door has no measurable effect below saturation and a comparatively weak effect above it. Using the label in allocation works: DCS-P0 reduces priority passengers' waits by 30\% to 43\% at $x\le1$ at a cost of 0.6 to 4.1~s to others and no appreciable change in the all-passenger mean. It is not a uniformly better controller. Under sustained overload it raises the all-passenger capped mean by 29.9~s at $x=1.3$ and increases the share of general passengers waiting more than five minutes from 11\% to 18\%. DCS-P0 is suitable where demand stays at or below the level at which destination control saturates, and unsuitable as a fixed setting where long overload periods occur. An adaptive rule that falls back to DCS-P1 when many passengers are waiting halved the overload cost in an exploratory test; this direction needs a dedicated study.

\emph{Q3.} In this design the served-passenger average and the all-arrival capped mean rank controllers alike. The capped mean is still the safer primary outcome above saturation, because it is defined for every arrival and its value does not depend on how many passengers happen to be served.

\subsection{Implications}
Two points follow for practice. First, under landing-call control the costs of priority-first boarding are concentrated in heavily loaded periods, generally remain small below the reference demand, and grow roughly in proportion to the share of priority passengers; an operator can adopt the policy on that basis. Second, an operator of a destination-control system should not expect a boarding rule to help in normal operation: the priority label has to enter the allocation. A further point concerns planning: passengers who need three places reduce handling capacity for everyone by an amount several times larger than any effect of boarding order (21.7~s against 3.4~s at the reference demand), and this is driven by space more than by transfer time. Where such passengers are expected in numbers, capacity planning should count them in places, not in persons. These statements rest on a model with illustrative parameters and indicate direction and relative size, not design values.

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{No empirical validation.} The model is verified and checked against classical theory, but not against measured data or an independent published benchmark.
\item \emph{Baselines.} The controllers are transparent implementations of published principles. Commercial dispatchers and learning-based controllers \cite{crites1998,wan2024,wang2021} were not implemented.
\item \emph{Traffic and buildings.} Single lobby, equal floor populations, single arrivals, no zoning; steady demand or one simple time-varying profile.
\item \emph{Priority passengers.} The label is assumed known and correct, and compliance with boarding order is assumed. The wheelchair-like profile uses illustrative values for space and time and represents only one kind of access requirement.
\item \emph{Measure and window.} $R_{600}$ does not describe waits beyond 600~s and understates effects where many waits reach the cap; levels at $x>1$ under steady demand depend on the measured window.
\item \emph{Parameters of DCS-P.} Only $\alpha^{P}\in\{0,1\}$ and one adaptive threshold were examined. The penalty $M$ and the ETDQ parameters were fixed on pilot streams without sensitivity analysis.
\item \emph{Inference.} Twenty replications per cell (ten in the supplementary experiments); intervals are unadjusted for multiplicity and reflect replication variability only. The supplementary experiments are exploratory.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
A verified, seconds-based lift group simulator was used to compare five group controllers, two boarding rules and two priority-passenger profiles in 31,680 paired runs, with every measured passenger followed long enough for a 600~s capped wait to be observed exactly.

Priority-first boarding under landing-call control costs other passengers at most 3.3~s in any cell below the reference demand, 1.8~s on average at it and 14.5~s at 1.15 times it, while priority passengers gain 9.3~s and 81.1~s respectively. Under destination control the same boarding rule has no measurable effect up to the reference demand. A priority-aware allocation rule, DCS-P0, reduces priority passengers' waits there by 30\% to 43\% for a cost of 0.6 to 4.1~s to others, but under sustained overload it raises the all-passenger mean substantially and should not be used as a fixed setting in such conditions. Passengers who need more space lower capacity for all by considerably more than boarding order changes anyone's wait. The served-passenger average and the capped mean ranked controllers identically in 659 of 660 comparisons.

Further work should validate the model against measured traffic, examine intermediate and adaptive settings of the allocation weight, and test the policies against stronger dispatchers.
